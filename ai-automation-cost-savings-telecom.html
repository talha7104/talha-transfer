On Monday, Deutsche Telekom put a financial target on its AI and automation strategy. The company expects the technologies to contribute around €2.5 billion in indirect-cost savings by 2030, measured against a 2023 baseline, with gross savings of around €1.1 billion targeted for 2027. Reuters reported the figures from the company's statement.

The important point isn't the size of the number. It's that management attached a baseline, financial targets, and dates to the program.

And the important qualification: these are targets, not realized savings. The program involves both AI and automation — so the figures shouldn't be read as savings attributable to generative AI or AI agents alone.

Where the €2.5 billion is supposed to come from

The company is applying AI where work is high-volume and repetitive. According to The Wall Street Journal: network operations, customer service, software development, and administrative processes. CEO Tim Hoettges said AI is being used to improve networks and services while supporting new business models.

Two operational examples make the cost case concrete: AI detecting peak cellular-network traffic earlier, and AI supporting customer-service representatives in resolving issues. (Reuters)

The relevant pattern is volume. When a task repeats across thousands or millions of interactions, small reductions in handling time, manual work, or delay become financially significant. That's more useful for planning than asking where an AI assistant would give the most impressive demo. The better question: where is repetitive work frequent enough for an improvement to compound?

It's not just a cost story — it's a revenue one too

The company is also attaching targets to AI-related revenue: from around €250M in 2026 to ~€800M by 2030, per the WSJ. The revenue plan includes several enterprise AI offerings:

  • A platform for automating recurring business tasks
  • A real-time AI assistant for phone calls — translating, answering, summarizing
  • T-AI Agent Platform (mid-sized companies) and T-AI Agentic Hub (larger organizations, public sector) — offerings to integrate autonomous agents into business workflows (ad-hoc-news)

Internal savings and external revenue are separate parts of the strategy — the sources don't establish that customer products were converted from internal automation projects. But the combination is worth noting: AI investment can be evaluated against more than one financial outcome. Reduced operating cost, additional revenue — or both.

Why this announcement is different from the usual AI PR

Most AI announcements emphasize capabilities. This one specifies financial objectives — and does it in a way that's actually auditable:

It has a baseline. Savings measured against 2023. A baseline gives management something concrete to compare future operating costs against — without it, "savings" is storytelling.

It has intermediate milestones. ~€1.1B gross for 2027, ~€2.5B indirect-cost savings for 2030. Dates make progress evaluable instead of aspirational.

It connects deployment to commercial products. Agent systems aren't presented only as internal experiments — they're also offerings for business customers, which coincides with a wider run of commercial agent launches (NoloWiz's weekly roundup).

None of this proves the targets will be hit. But it shows what investors and operators can measure: baseline cost, implementation milestones, operating use cases, revenue targets.

What this means for your business

You don't need a €2.5 billion target to use the same measurement discipline:

Point automation at volume, not novelty. Start with processes that recur frequently: service requests, document handling, reporting, internal routing, data entry. A low-volume workflow may not justify implementation and operating cost even if it's technically easy to automate.

Separate gross savings from net savings. Reducing manual effort isn't reducing total cost. Include implementation, model usage, infrastructure, integration, monitoring, human review, maintenance, and exception handling in the business case.

Give every initiative a baseline and a target. Measure the current process before changing it: transaction volume, labor time, processing time, error rates, current operating cost. Then define the outcome the automation is expected to improve.

Don't attribute everything to AI. The €2.5B target combines AI and automation. Your program may combine workflow software, rules, integrations, conventional automation, and language models. Measure the system as it operates — don't assign every improvement to the AI component.

Keep accountability explicit. The customer-service example shows one workable model: AI supports representatives while people retain responsibility for the interaction. For higher-risk workflows, define which actions run automatically, which need approval, and how decisions get reviewed.

A 90-day path to your own savings number

A smaller organization can apply the same measurement principles without the telecom's scale:

  • Days 1–14: map the volume. List recurring processes; record frequency, staff time consumed, and where errors or delays create cost.
  • Days 15–30: baseline one process. Pick a high-volume candidate and document its current economics — labor plus the systems and operating costs that matter to the comparison.
  • Days 31–75: build one working automation. Keep scope narrow. Automate one clearly defined process, integrate with the systems it needs, add a human approval step where consequences justify one.
  • Days 76–90: compare against the baseline. Measure processing time, manual effort, operating cost, and quality. Include the recurring cost of running the automation itself.

If the economics work, the result becomes evidence for the next process. If they don't, you've learned that before committing to a broader rollout. That's the practical lesson behind a large financial target: AI automation gets easier to manage when the discussion moves from capabilities to measurable operating outcomes.

FAQ

Is Deutsche Telekom really saving €2.5 billion with AI?

Not yet — it's a target, not a realized result. The company expects AI and automation to contribute ~€2.5B in indirect-cost savings by 2030 vs. a 2023 baseline (~€1.1B gross by 2027). Note it combines AI and conventional automation, not generative AI alone.

Where are the savings supposed to come from?

High-volume repetitive work: network operations (e.g., earlier peak-traffic detection), customer service (AI-assisted representatives), software development, and administrative processes. The pattern is volume — small per-unit gains compounding across millions of interactions.

What's the revenue side of the plan?

AI-related revenue targeted from ~€250M (2026) to ~€800M (2030), via enterprise offerings including the T-AI Agent Platform (mid-market) and T-AI Agentic Hub (enterprise/public sector).

How can a smaller business copy this?

Use the measurement discipline, not the scale: baseline one high-volume process, separate gross from net savings (include implementation, monitoring, review costs), set dated targets, and run a 90-day map → baseline → build → compare cycle before expanding.
